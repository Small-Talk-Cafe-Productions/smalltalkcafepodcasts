Causative Constructions: fare / lasciare + Infinitive

Basic Structure of Fare + Infinitive

To have something done / to make someone do something

📌 Nota grammaticale

The verb 'fare' combined with an infinitive indicates that the subject causes an action to happen, either by performing it themselves through an agent or by ordering someone else to do it. The structure is: 'fare' + direct infinitive without any prepositions.

Esempio

Basic Structure of Lasciare + Infinitive

To let / to allow something to happen

📌 Nota grammaticale

Similar to 'fare', 'lasciare' is followed directly by an infinitive without a preposition. It expresses permission, allowance, or the act of leaving things as they are. It translates to 'to let' or 'to allow' in English.

Esempio

Agent Expression with 'da' (+ Noun)

Expressing who performs the action using 'da'

📌 Nota grammaticale

When the agent (the person actually doing the action) is expressed as a noun or a specific person after 'fare', it must be introduced by the preposition 'da'. This clarifies who carries out the causative request.

Esempio

Object Pronouns with Causative Verbs

Placement of unstressed object pronouns (mi, ti, lo, la...)

📌 Nota grammaticale

With 'fare' and 'lasciare', object pronouns relating to the infinitive generally precede the conjugated causative verb (proclisis), forming a single verbal unit. Alternatively, they can attach to the infinitive by dropping its final 'e'.

Esempio

Intransitive vs. Transitive Agent Pronouns (gli, le, loro vs. mi, ti)

Using indirect pronouns for intransitive verbs and direct for transitive

📌 Nota grammaticale

When the agent of the infinitive is expressed as a pronoun, the choice depends on whether the infinitive is transitive or intransitive. For transitive verbs with an explicit direct object, the agent takes an indirect pronoun (gli, le, loro, ti, mi).

Esempio

Intransitive Infinitives and Direct Pronouns

Agent pronouns with intransitive verbs

📌 Nota grammaticale

If the infinitive verb is intransitive (it takes no direct object, like andare, venire, uscire), the person performing the action acts as the direct object of 'fare'. Therefore, direct object pronouns (mi, ti, lo, la, ci, vi, li, le) must be used.

Esempio

Compound Tenses and Auxiliary Selection (Passato Prossimo)

Choosing 'avere' vs. 'essere' in past tenses with causative fare

📌 Nota grammaticale

In compound tenses like the passato prossimo, the auxiliary verb for the causative construction is almost always avere, regardless of whether the dependent infinitive is transitive or intransitive. The past participle 'fatto' remains invariable unless a direct object pronoun precedes it.

Esempio

Agreement of Past Participle with Preceding Direct Pronouns

Feminine/plural agreement of 'fatto' with fronted direct objects

📌 Nota grammaticale

When a direct object pronoun (lo, la, li, le) precedes the compound causative verb, the past participle 'fatto' agrees in gender and number with that pronoun. Note that third-person indirect pronouns (gli/le) do not trigger agreement.

Esempio

Reflexive Verbs in Causative Constructions

Handling reflexive pronouns with fare / lasciare

📌 Nota grammaticale

When the action performed by the agent reflects back on the agent (reflexive verbs), the reflexive pronoun either changes its position or merges into the sentence structure. Often, with 'fare', the reflexive pronoun is omitted if the context makes the possession obvious (e.g., farsi tagliare i capelli = to have one's hair cut).

Esempio

Nuance: Fare vs. Lasciare (Active Causation vs. Permission)

Distinguishing active commission from passive allowance

📌 Nota grammaticale

At B2 level, mastery requires knowing the precise pragmatic difference: 'fare + infinitive' means actively causing, ordering, or arranging for something to happen. 'Lasciare + infinitive' means permitting, tolerating, or refraining from preventing an action.

Esempio

Idiomatic Expressions with 'Farsi' (farsi capire, farsi vivo)

Fixed idiomatic causative phrases for advanced communication

📌 Nota grammaticale

Italian uses several reflexive causative idioms with 'farsi' that are essential for B2 fluency. Examples include 'farsi capire' (to make oneself understood), 'farsi vivo' (to make contact / show up), and 'farsi pregare' (to play hard to get / be reluctant).

Esempio

Passive Nuance in Causative Constructions (Implicit Agents)

Implied passive sense when the agent is omitted

📌 Nota grammaticale

When the agent is omitted in a 'fare + infinitive' structure, the construction takes on a strong passive meaning: the subject has something done to them by an unspecified or contextual agent (e.g., having a service performed).
